# Supply-Chain-Carbon-Emission-Factors-Analysis
The goal of this analysis is to explore the distribution of greenhouse gas emission factors across different industrial sectors to identify which industries contribute most to supply chain emissions per dollar spent. Looking at this question allows us to find areas that impact the environment the most. The main question is which industrial sectors, categorized by NAICS codes, have the highest supply chain emission factors? Many organizations are beginning to prioritize net zero carbon emissions, understanding their carbon emissions helps address areas for improvement. This data helps identify places where spending leads to disproportional high emissions. This information would be important to procurement managers and environmental policy planners who would use this for emissions reporting and sustainable sourcing strategies.

The dataset came from the United States Data.gov from their data catalog. Each row represents a certain industrial activity that is categorized by the 2017 North American Industry Classification System (NAICS). The 2017 NAICS Title is the descriptive name of the industry. Supply Chain Emission Factors with Margins represents the total carbon intensity (kg CO2e) per 2022 USD price and GHGs are greenhouse gas emission factors. The dataset before cleaning and refining contains 1,016 rows and 8 columns. The data assumes a 2022 price level and U.S. average production methods. It also doesn’t capture differences between specific companies or geographic differences outside the U.S..

The data was imported using pandas for efficient manipulation. Performed a check to verify the dataset size. Margin columns individually were dropped since using “supply_chain_emission_factors_with_margins” includes the data to give total carbon intensity and emissions. Renamed columns to lowercase with underscores, example being total_factor, for easier coding and to prevent syntax errors. When handling NAICS codes they were converted to strings as treating them as numbers would delete leading zeros and break the categorization, having them as strings prevents leading zeros from being dropped. Dropped the “GHG” and “Reference USEEIO Code” columns as it makes the dataframe much easier to read since every single row contained the same value. Grouped data by creating a Sector column by taking the first two digits of the NAICS code and linking them to names. This was necessary as 1,016 individual industries were too many to visualize effectively.

Visualizations Created:
Top 20 Hotspots Bar Chart: A horizontal bar chart of the industries with the highest emission factors.
Average Emission by Sector: A bar chart showing the mean of major economic sectors such as Agriculture and Mining.
The reason for these visualizations is that the Top 20 chart answers which specific industry is the worst by having the highest carbon emission question and the Sectoral chart provides a summary for policy planning based on general sectors. From these graphs it is found that agriculture is consistently the most intensive sector. Most industries have low emission factors such as under 0.2, but a small number of industries, like Beef Cattle or Coal, have factors that are 10 to 20 times higher than the average. What is surprising is that some sectors like Software have significant margins that represent over 50% of their total footprint, even though their total footprint is small.

The data suggests that not all spending is equal in terms of carbon emission impact. If a procurement manager was to spend $1,000 on "Software" then they are responsible for about 80kg of CO2e. If they spend $1,000 on "Beef Cattle" then they are responsible for nearly 2,900kg of CO2e. This effectively shows how carbon emission impact can vary based on what you're spending on. So to reach net zero organizations shouldn’t just buy less but rather they should buy differently. Shifting a corporation's spending away from high intensity sectors towards lower intensity sectors is the fastest way to reduce a supply chain's carbon footprint. It would be misleading to say an industry is clean just because its factor is low. A sector such as “Construction” has a medium factor but since the total global spend on construction is trillions of dollars its total contribution to climate change is high.

The data is based on U.S. averages so this could create a bias where a company using efficient renewable energy is penalized by being assigned the average factor for their industry. It also ignores use-phase emissions such as a car’s emissions from being used after purchase. We assume that 2022 USD values stay a stable proxy for carbon intensity, but inflation or price spikes in certain commodities could cause disparities. What was surprising was how much the "Margins" affected certain industries. We often think of the factory as being the main source of pollution and carbon emissions but for many consumer goods the retail and transport chain is the most impactful. If there had been more data, you could combine this with global trade data to see how these factors change when goods are imported from countries with less regulated power grids.

U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. (2024). Supply Chain Greenhouse Gas Emission Factors v1.3 by NAICS-6 [Data set]. U.S. EPA Office of Research and Development. https://doi.org/10.23719/1531143
